How the Milky Way got its spin

Gaia and APOGEE have directly observed three phases in the kinematic evolution of our galaxy: a disordered protogalaxy, a hot, thick disk, and a cold, thin disk. I use FIRE-2 cosmological zoom-in simulations of Milky Way-mass galaxies to study the dynamics and physics of these phases.

The Milky Way remembers

For the most part, stars maintain the kinematic properties they were born with, and each new generation of stars forms from gas that has been continuously enriched with metals such as iron. A star’s orbit and chemistry therefore record how and when it formed.

Gaia and APOGEE measure the motions and chemical abundances of millions of stars in the Milky Way. These surveys have revealed three phases in its history. FIRE-2 simulations of Milky Way-mass galaxies reproduce all three, and they show where each generation of stars sits today.

Three phases

The images show Romeo, one of the twelve simulated galaxies, edge-on in each phase.

Protogalaxy

Star formation rate is low and bursty and the conversion rate of gas into stars is also low. The galaxy baryons (gas and stars) slosh around the galaxy's center of mass. The galaxy has no net angular momentum. The potential is not centrally concentrated, and its shape is chaotic.

Thick disk

The galaxy acquires a defined dynamical center and the sloshing ends. The baryons become well-centered in the total potential, and the gas begins to rotate coherently. Then, stars later also begin to acquire a net rotation. The star formation peaks and remains bursty, although the conversion rate of gas into stars peaks in this era. The disk begins its formation while the potential is most unconcentrated, and it only becomes centrally concentrated, plateauing a few Gyr after the disk begins its formation.

Thin disk

The thin disk is triggered by the virialization of the inner circumgalactic medium, the gas surrounding the galaxy. Star formation rates become steady in this phase, and the conversion rate of gas into stars is at an intermediate value. The youngest stars form on cold, nearly circular orbits. The potential is very centrally concentrated.

The three phases in motion

A toy model that animates the sequence with simple rules. In the protogalaxy, cold gas streams accrete asymmetrically along filaments, small satellite galaxies merge constantly, and bursts of star formation blow hot gas back out — the galaxy is in a state of disequilibrium. Once the baryonic center is well-defined as gas and stars stop sloshing within the total potential, a clumpy, hot thick disk emerges, which then later transitions into a cool thin disk.

Twelve galaxies

Simulated Milky Way-mass galaxies experience the same three phases.

Why disks form when they do

The full suite of FIRE-2 Milky Way-mass galaxies forms disks under the same physical conditions. Disks emerge at no particular gas fraction, stellar mass or halo mass, and mergers play no systematic role in either transition.

Baryonic settling precedes disk formation

In the protogalaxy, the baryons slosh as the center of mass of the gas and stars moves chaotically within the total potential. The gas begins to rotate coherently only once this motion settles and the galaxy has a well-defined dynamical center, which makes baryonic settling a prerequisite for disk formation.

The movie shows Thelma, whose gas disk emerges 7.8 Gyr ago. In its protogalaxy phase, the bulk motion of its baryons (stars and gas) changes direction violently every few hundred million years, and sb rises and falls sharply. After the gas disk forms, these bulk motions stay low.

Gas disks form before stellar disks

Cool gas acquires coherent rotation first, and the rotational support of newly formed stars rises only after a delay of a few hundred Myr to more than 3 Gyr in some systems. Milky Way progenitors therefore pass through a phase in which a rotationally supported cool-gas disk coexists with kinematically hot, weakly rotating young stars.

Thelma has the longest delay between the emergence of its gas disk and the emergence of its stellar disk in the suite. Its gas rotates as a disk 3.2 Gyr before its stars do. In every galaxy, the gas disk forms before the stars, and cooldown comes after the stellar disk forms. The formation time of the stellar disk and the cooldown time are only weakly correlated, which points to distinct physics behind the two transitions.

Least concentrated as the gas disk emerges

The central potential is least concentrated, with a rising rotation curve, as the gas disk emerges, and it reaches its highest concentration before cooldown. This sequence challenges models in which a concentrated center triggers disk formation.

Inner-CGM virialization triggers cooldown

The thin-disk phase begins when the inner circumgalactic medium (CGM) virializes, and star formation becomes steady at the same time. A thin disk requires gas to accrete slowly enough for its angular momentum to mix and become coherent before it joins the galaxy.

The temperature maps of Romeo show this transition. Before cooldown, cool and warm gas fills much of the inner halo. After cooldown, the inner CGM is hot, and cool gas lies mainly in the disk. In Romeo, Romulus and Thelma, cooldown coincides with the rise in the ratio of the gas cooling time to the free-fall time that marks the virialization of the inner CGM.

The physics governing the emergence of a galaxy disk In preparation

I measure the gravitational torques on gas and stars as galaxies form their disks in an effort to pin down the dynamical origin of the Milky Way's angular momentum structure.

In the protogalaxy, the baryons have no well-defined center, and their bulk motions within the total potential are large. Non-axisymmetric torques from clumps, satellites and anisotropic accretion are strong enough to rearrange the angular momentum of the cool and star-forming gas within a dynamical time or two. I measure their strength with the scrambling parameter, the fractional change in a population’s angular momentum that these torques could produce in one dynamical time. The rotation of the star-forming gas is misaligned with that of the stars, at times counter-rotating, and the binding energy of the stars is weak compared with the energy that recent supernovae inject.

The bulk motions then die down, and the baryons settle into the total potential. The torques on the cool gas weaken until they would need two or more dynamical times to rearrange its angular momentum, so coherent orbits survive and the gas disk emerges. The star-forming gas, from which stars are born, still feels strong torques and remains misaligned with the stars. The binding energy of the stars grows but stays below the supernova energy.

The torques on the star-forming gas then weaken as well, and the binding energy of the stars overtakes the energy that recent supernovae inject, so the inner galaxy is bound against feedback. The stellar disk forms, and the star-forming gas aligns with the stars.

Early disks seen by ALMA and JWST In preparation

ALMA has found unexpectedly cold, early disks by measuring gas kinematics. JWST finds many disk-like shapes in early galaxies, identified from imaging and axis ratios. I build mock JWST and ALMA observations of FIRE galaxies and compare them with true kinematics, which we can get from simulation data, to determine how often the images reveal genuine rotating disks.

In FIREbox, gas disks form before stellar disks across the whole galaxy population. At z = 4, 62% of galaxies have rotationally supported gas, while only 2% have rotationally supported stars, and at no redshift does a galaxy host a stellar disk without a gas disk. ALMA cold-gas kinematics and JWST imaging therefore probe different stages of disk assembly.

Chemical fingerprints In preparation

Each phase leaves its own chemical and kinematic signature in stars and gas. I study FIRE-2 chemical abundances to make observable predictions about the Milky Way's archaeology and evolutionary history.

The iron abundance [Fe/H] rises as each generation of stars forms, so it orders Romeo’s stars by age. Stars born in the protogalaxy have [Fe/H] below about −1, the thick disk forms between −1 and 0, and the thin disk forms near the solar value.

Magnesium and oxygen come mainly from core-collapse supernovae, which explode soon after stars form, while Type Ia supernovae add iron over billions of years. [Mg/Fe] and [O/Fe] therefore fall with time, and orbital circularity jumps over a narrow range of both as the stellar disk forms. A small population of old stars, under 1% of the total, sits near [Mg/Fe] = 0 and [O/Fe] = 0 on hot orbits.

[C/N] falls steeply during the thick-disk phase and then levels off. Every star born after cooldown has nearly the same [C/N], so [C/N] separates the thin disk cleanly from older stars while saying little about the ages of thin-disk stars. Of the four ratios, [Mg/Fe] and [O/Fe] mark the formation of the stellar disk most sharply, a prediction that Gaia and APOGEE abundances can check directly.

I carry out this work as a member of the FIRE collaboration (Feedback In Realistic Environments), whose cosmological simulations model star formation and stellar feedback in detail.
